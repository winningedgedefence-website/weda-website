Fifteen Things Parents Believe About Sainik School Admissions That Simply Are Not True

WEDA Parent Knowledge Series Col Amardeep Sir, SM (Retd.) 12 min read

Most of what circulates during admission season is wrong. Some of it is expensively wrong.

The Season of Confident Advice

Every year around the time notifications appear, a particular kind of message begins to circulate. It arrives in a family WhatsApp group, forwarded from someone's cousin's neighbour, and it is always stated with complete certainty.

Girls can't apply. You need coaching or there's no point. It's reserved for Army families. Once the child is in, the NDA is settled.

I have been on the receiving end of these conversations for years now. What strikes me is not that parents believe wrong things — everyone does, about most subjects. It is that the wrong things are believed so confidently, and acted on so decisively.

A family decides not to apply at all because of something a relative said. Another prepares a child for the wrong examination entirely. A third pours money into coaching a child who did not need it, while neglecting the thing that actually decides the outcome.

None of these families lacked effort. They lacked accurate information, and the accurate information was freely available the whole time.

So this is a plain list of fifteen beliefs I encounter most often, and what is actually the case.

Part One: Who Can Actually Apply

Myth 1. "These schools are really for Army families."

A defence quota does exist. I want to state that plainly, because guides that pretend otherwise are being dishonest in the opposite direction.

A share of seats at Sainik Schools is reserved for the wards of serving and retired Armed Forces personnel, and at Rashtriya Military Schools that share is very much larger. If you are a defence family, this works in your favour and you should know it.

Now the number nobody quotes correctly. People say the defence quota is 25%. It is 25% of the balance remaining after the SC, ST and OBC reservations — which works out to roughly 13 seats in every 100. In a school offering 100 Class VI seats, a typical distribution runs: General 38, OBC-NCL 27, SC 15, Defence 13, ST 7.

About 87 seats in every 100 are open to civilian candidates. Civilian children are admitted every single year, in large numbers, and always have been. Sainik Schools were founded in the first place to widen the pool from which the officer corps was drawn — to bring in families who had no military connection at all. Turning them into a closed shop for service families would defeat the entire purpose of their existence.

Rashtriya Military Schools are the genuine exception, and there the myth is closer to the truth. Around 70% of RMS seats are reserved for the wards of JCOs and Other Ranks, with the remaining 30% shared between officers' wards and civilian candidates. If you are a civilian family, treat RMS as one option among several rather than your main route.

Apply anyway. At Sainik Schools the quota is real and small. The open seat is real and large.

Myth 2. "Girls cannot apply."

They can, and this myth is quietly costing families more than any other on this list.

Girl cadets were first admitted on a pilot basis at Sainik School Chhingchhip in Mizoram, and from the 2021-22 academic session all thirty-three Sainik Schools began admitting girls. Each school reserves 10% of its Class VI strength or 10 seats, whichever is higher, for girls — and, this matters, girls are ranked on a separate merit list rather than competing against boys for the same places. At Class IX there is no fixed percentage; availability is set out in the vacancy tables each cycle.

Girls are also admitted to Rashtriya Military Schools and to RIMC. Women have been eligible for the National Defence Academy since 2021.

I still meet parents of capable daughters who never applied because someone told them not to bother.

Myth 3. "Sainik School, Military School and RIMC are all the same thing."

This is the most expensive myth on the list, and it is not in most versions of this article.

They are three separate systems. Sainik Schools admit at Class VI and Class IX through AISSEE. Rashtriya Military Schools run an entirely different examination, the RMS CET, which includes a personal interview. RIMC admits to Class VIII only, twice a year, through its own examination and its own application route via your state.

Different notifications. Different papers. Different selection stages. A child prepared for one is not prepared for another.

Every year some family discovers this in the final week. Confirm which institution you are applying to before a single hour of preparation begins.

Myth 4. "Children from English-medium schools have the advantage."

For Class VI entry, the paper is offered in thirteen languages — English, Hindi and a range of regional languages. A child who thinks in Marathi or Bengali or Tamil can sit the paper in that language.

The Class IX paper is in English. That is worth knowing early if your child is heading for the later entry point, because it is a gap you can close with two years' notice and cannot close in two months.

So the honest answer is: medium of instruction matters for one entry point and not the other. Check which one applies to you.

Part Two: How Selection Actually Works

This is the section I would ask you to read most carefully, because the popular understanding of it is not merely incomplete. It is backwards.

Myth 5. "Marks aren't everything — they look at the whole child."

For Sainik School admission, marks very nearly are everything.

There is no interview in the Sainik School selection process. No panel assesses your child's leadership, bearing or character. The sequence is: written examination, then merit ranking, then e-counselling, then medical examination, then verification of documents. That is the whole of it.

I say this without pleasure, because discipline and character are what these schools are for, and it is a real irony that neither is measured at the gate. But a parent who believes an all-round profile will carry a weak paper has misunderstood the process, and will find out too late.

Note the distinction from RMS and RIMC, where an interview genuinely is part of selection. Three institutions, three processes.

Myth 6. "There's a national cut-off. If you cross it, you're in."

There is no single national merit list.

Ranking is worked out school-wise, class-wise and category-wise. Your child is competing for a specific number of seats, at a specific school, within a specific category — not against every candidate in India.

The practical consequence is that two children with identical marks can have entirely different outcomes, depending on which school and which pool they sit in. It also means a "cut-off" quoted to you by anyone who does not specify the school, class, category and year is a number without meaning.

Read the school-wise vacancy tables in the appendices of the current information bulletin. Almost nobody opens them. They are where the actual competition is described.

Myth 7. "Physical fitness is what really counts."

There is no physical fitness test in the Sainik School entrance examination. None. A child who runs well gains nothing at this stage.

What does exist is a medical fitness examination, and it comes after the written result. It is applied seriously, and children are disqualified at it every year.

These two things are constantly confused. Fitness will not get your child in. A medical condition can keep them out. If there is a known issue — vision, hearing, anything on record — find out where you stand early rather than at the final stage, when nothing can be done about it.

Myth 8. "Getting into a Sainik School means the NDA is settled."

It means nothing of the sort, and I want to be blunt because this belief shapes families' entire outlook for six years.

NDA selection is open competition through the Union Public Service Commission. There is no quota, preference or advantage of any kind for alumni of Sainik Schools, Rashtriya Military Schools or RIMC. Every candidate sits the same written examination, faces the same Services Selection Board, and passes the same medical.

What these schools give a child is an environment — structure, physical habits, early exposure to the idea of service, and the company of others pointed the same way. That is a genuine advantage in preparation. It is not a shortcut in selection, and any institution that suggests otherwise is selling you something.

Part Three: Myths About Preparing

Myth 9. "Coaching is compulsory."

It is not. These papers test the school syllabus of the preceding classes, and children clear them every year from ordinary schools with nothing more than good textbooks, consistent work and a parent paying attention.

Coaching helps a specific kind of student: one who needs external structure, or who lacks access to decent practice material, or who has a particular weak area needing focused work. Those are real needs, and where they exist, coaching earns its cost.

But it is a tool, not a requirement. I run a coaching academy and I will tell you the same thing I tell parents who walk into my office: if your child is disciplined and you can supervise, you may not need us.

One thing worth knowing if you do decide to look. The sector is now regulated. The Ministry of Education's Guidelines for Regulation of Coaching Centre (2024) address misleading promises and guarantees of rank or marks, and the Central Consumer Protection Authority's 2024 guidelines on coaching advertisements prohibit claims such as "100% selection" — with penalties under the Consumer Protection Act. They also bar the use of a selected student's name or photograph without written consent obtained after the result. Read the next brochure you are handed with that in mind.

Myth 10. "Only exceptionally bright children get in."

The children I have watched succeed at this were not, for the most part, remarkable. They were consistent.

They sat down at the same time each day. They finished what they started. They redid the questions they got wrong instead of moving on to easier ones. Over eight or ten months, that ordinary discipline outperformed cleverness that showed up in bursts.

If your child is capable of steady work, they are capable of this examination.

Myth 11. "More books mean better preparation."

A shelf of unfinished books is worse than one book worked through twice.

Choose a small number of good resources — ideally aligned to the official syllabus — and finish them properly. The instinct to buy more is usually anxiety looking for something to do.

Myth 12. "Mock tests are for children who are already doing well."

Backwards. A weaker student needs them more.

Mock tests are not a measurement exercise, they are a training exercise. They teach a child to manage a clock, to leave a question and come back, to sit still for two and a half hours, and to recover from a bad first section instead of collapsing into it. None of that is learned from a textbook, and all of it is worth marks.

Start them earlier than feels comfortable.

Myth 13. "Six or seven hours a day is what it takes."

For a ten-year-old, six hours of study is not dedication. It is usually two hours of work and four hours of sitting at a desk feeling guilty.

Ninety focused minutes, at a fixed time, every day, will do more over eight months than long unfocused sessions that leave a child dreading the table. Consistency is the variable that matters. Duration is the one parents can see, which is why it gets over-managed.

Part Four: Myths About What It All Means

Myth 14. "If this doesn't work out, the child isn't cut out for it."

Every child develops on a different schedule, and a single examination taken at ten or thirteen measures a narrow slice of a person on one particular morning.

Some children who did not clear it went on to the NDA by another route. Others found their direction somewhere else entirely and are doing well. A result is information about a paper. It is not a verdict on a child, and children take their reading of it directly from how their parents react.

Be careful what you let a result mean in your house.

Myth 15. "This only makes sense if the child joins the Armed Forces."

Most alumni of these schools do not end up in uniform. They become doctors, engineers, administrators, scientists, people running businesses.

What they carry out with them is the habit of order and the assumption that they are responsible for other people. That is worth having in any life.

If you would regard the education as wasted unless it ends in a commission, I would ask you to reconsider before you apply — for your child's sake more than your own. A child who senses that only one outcome will satisfy you carries that for six years.

The One Habit That Prevents All of This

Everything above comes from the same source: information that travelled fast and was never checked.

So build a single habit this season.

Before acting on any piece of admission advice, ask whether you can verify it through an official source.

For anything touching eligibility, documents, deadlines, examinations or procedure, the current official notification is the only authority that counts. Not a forwarded message, not a coaching brochure, not a well-meaning relative, and not this article either. Go to the Sainik Schools Society, to the examination portal, to the school itself.

It takes ten minutes. It prevents almost every mistake described above.

A Last Word

In the Army I learned that most failures are not caused by insufficient effort. They are caused by acting on bad information with complete confidence.

That is exactly what happens to families during admission season. The effort is nearly always there. What goes missing is the ten minutes it would have taken to check.

Give your child accurate information and steady support, and let the preparation do its work. That is the whole of your job, and it is enough.

Col Amardeep Sir, SM (Retd.)Founder & Director, The Winning Edge Defence Academy

Editorial note

This article deliberately avoids specific dates, fees, age limits, reservation percentages and document specifications, all of which change from one admission cycle to another.
