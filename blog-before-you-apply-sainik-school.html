Before You Fill Any Sainik School Application Form Read This First

WEDA Parent Knowledge Series — Issue 01 Col Amardeep Sir, SM (Retd.) 9 min read

The form takes an hour to complete. The preparation behind it starts much earlier.

The Question Outside The School Office

A father sat outside a school office with a folder resting on his lap. Birth certificate, Aadhaar, school records, recent photographs — every document in order, every page in sequence. By any reasonable measure, he was ready.

Then he asked a question that had clearly been troubling him for some time.

"Have I prepared my child, or have I only prepared the paperwork?"

I have thought about that question often since. It is the right one to ask, and very few parents ask it early enough.

Every year, thousands of families across India begin this process with real hope. They download notifications, gather certificates, compare eligibility conditions, mark dates on the calendar. All of it is necessary. None of it is sufficient.

The application form is not the start of your child's preparation. It is the first visible milestone in something that began much earlier — in the information you chose to trust, the habits you encouraged, and the atmosphere you created at home.

Families who move through admission season calmly are rarely the ones who finish first. They are usually the ones who started quietly, months before the portal opened.

This guide will not teach you how to fill a form. The official notification does that better than any article can. What it will do is help you approach the whole process with fewer avoidable mistakes and a clearer head.

From The Desk Of Col Amardeep Sir, SM (Retd.)

During my years in the Army, I learned something that has stayed with me long after I hung up the uniform.

Preparation is almost never visible.

People notice the parade. They notice the medals and the turnout. What they do not see are the hours of planning, rehearsal and correction that made those minutes possible. The visible moment is always the smallest part of the work.

The same holds true for a child's education. Parents want the best for their children, and in that effort the application form becomes the centre of attention. It feels urgent. It has a deadline attached. It is something you can do.

But the form is administration. Preparation is something else entirely, and it starts earlier.

Over the years I have met a great many parents, all wanting the same thing. The families who came through the process most confidently were rarely the ones with the greatest resources. They were the ones who prepared with patience.

Col Amardeep Sir, SM (Retd.)Founder & Director, The Winning Edge Defence Academy

Four Institutions, Four Different Doors

Here is the single most common source of confusion I encounter, and it costs families entire admission cycles.

Parents use Sainik School, New Sainik School, Rashtriya Military School and RIMC as though they describe one thing. They do not. These are four different systems, with different entrance examinations, different entry classes and different selection stages.

Applying to the wrong one, or preparing for the wrong examination, is not a small error. It is a lost year.

Sainik Schools function under the Sainik Schools Society, Ministry of Defence. Entry is at Class VI and Class IX, through the All India Sainik Schools Entrance Examination — AISSEE — which the National Testing Agency conducts on the Society's behalf. Selection runs through the written examination, then e-counselling, then medical examination and document verification.

New Sainik Schools are part of the same Society but built differently. The Government has approved the setting up of a hundred such schools on an affiliation basis, in partnership with State governments, NGOs and private partners. Fee support is available from the Government for a portion of fees, subject to a ceiling. Two practical points matter here. They use the same AISSEE examination, so preparation is common. But availability differs school by school — a New Sainik School may be admitting at Class VI while not yet having reached Class IX. Check the appendices of the current information bulletin for the school you have in mind before you build any plan around it.

Rashtriya Military Schools are a separate system with their own Common Entrance Test — RMS CET — with entry at Class VI and Class IX. And there is a stage here that catches families out every year: RMS selection includes an interview, in addition to the written test and medical examination. Parents who prepare a child only for a written paper are preparing for two-thirds of the process.

Rashtriya Indian Military College, Dehradun is the narrowest door of the four. Entry is to Class VIII only. The examination is held twice a year, and roughly twenty-five cadets are admitted per term. Both boys and girls are eligible. Applications are routed through the candidate's State or Union Territory rather than through a single national portal — a procedural difference worth understanding well before you need it.

Four institutions. Four notifications. Read the correct one.

Where Your Information Comes From

Admission season brings a flood of advice — from relatives, from school gates, from WhatsApp groups, from social media accounts that exist mainly to collect followers.

Some of it is helpful. A good deal of it is last year's information, repeated with confidence.

Procedures, document requirements and timelines can change from one cycle to the next. So build one habit and keep it:

Before acting on any piece of admission advice, ask whether you can verify it through an official source.

For anything touching eligibility, documentation, deadlines or application procedure, the current official notification is the only authority. Coaching material, forwarded messages and articles — including this one — are commentary. Useful commentary, sometimes. Never a substitute.

The WEDA Parent Readiness Check™

Preparation is not one activity. It is a sequence of small decisions taken consistently.

By the time an application portal opens, well-prepared families have already done most of the work that matters. They understand the process, their documents are in order, their child has a study routine, and — most importantly — they are not deciding anything under pressure.

Six questions. Not a test. Just a way to pause before the next step.

1. Do we understand the whole process, or only the form?

Before collecting a single certificate, understand what the full sequence looks like for your chosen institution. What are the stages? What follows the written examination? What will your child need to be ready for six months from now?

Many parents plan only as far as submission and treat everything after it as a separate problem. Understanding the full sequence first makes every deadline afterwards easier to manage.

2. Have we read the current notification in full?

Not summarised. Not explained by someone else. Read.

Review eligibility conditions carefully, understand the instructions before you begin filling anything, and where something is unclear, seek clarification through official channels rather than assumption. Ten minutes with the notification prevents hours of confusion later.

3. Are our documents ready before we need them?

Families who start hunting for documents after the portal opens create pressure that was entirely avoidable.

Build one folder in advance. Check that names, dates and spellings are consistent across every record — inconsistencies between a birth certificate and a school record are among the most common causes of last-minute difficulty. Where uploads are required, prepare clean scans well before the closing date.

This is not about tidiness. It is about being free to support your child instead of searching for paperwork.

4. Have we started preparing the child, not just the file?

The most overlooked question of the six.

Some families wait until submission is complete before beginning academic preparation. There is rarely a reason to wait. Administrative work and academic work can run in parallel.

And preparation is not measured in hours. It is measured in consistency. Modest effort repeated across many weeks will beat intense effort concentrated in the final month, almost every time.

5. Have we planned the timeline?

Every season has its milestones. Families who mark them early experience noticeably less stress than those who begin planning in the last week.

Make a simple calendar. Allocate time for reading the notification, organising documents, reviewing the completed application, regular study, revision, and practice under timed conditions.

Planning does not remove problems. It creates room to absorb them.

6. Have we prepared ourselves?

Children read the room better than we credit them for. They notice the tone of conversations, the tension before deadlines, the silences after a poor practice score.

When parents approach the process with patience, children generally find confidence. When every conversation turns on marks, ranks and comparison, children start associating preparation with anxiety rather than learning.

A steady home does not guarantee a result. It gives a child the space to do their best work, which is all any of us can offer.

Small Oversights, Large Consequences

Most admission difficulties are not caused by insufficient effort. They are caused by details that nobody checked.

A name spelled differently across two documents. A photograph that does not meet specification. A field left blank on a form nobody reviewed a second time. A date remembered one day late.

Individually, none of these is dramatic. Together, they account for a significant share of the problems families face each year.

Good preparation is not pessimism. It is simply reducing the number of ways a preventable mistake can occur.

The Conversation Worth Having First

Before you discuss the examination with your child, have a different conversation.

Not about marks. Not about ranks. Not about which cousin cleared what.

Something closer to this:

"We are not asking you to be better than anyone else. We are asking you to be a little better than you were yesterday. Our job is to help you prepare honestly. The result will follow its own course."

Children remember conversations like that long after they have forgotten a score. Confidence grows where expectations are realistic and support does not fluctuate with performance.

And a related discipline for parents: do not compare your child with another child. Compare this week's preparation with last week's. One of those comparisons builds confidence. The other builds only anxiety.

A Final Word

In uniform, I learned that success rarely belongs to those who begin in a hurry. It belongs to those who prepare with patience, and who keep preparing when nobody is watching.

An application window stays open for a few weeks. The habits a child builds while preparing stay with them for years — through school, through the Academy if they go that way, and through whatever else they choose.

That is the part worth getting right.

If this guide helps one family replace uncertainty with clarity, it has done its work. I wish every parent, and every child, a steady season ahead.

Col Amardeep Sir, SM (Retd.)

Editorial note

This guide is written to remain useful across admission cycles. Eligibility criteria, application windows, fees, document specifications and examination schedules change from one academic session to another and are deliberately not reproduced here. Before acting on any procedural detail, refer to the latest official admission notification issued by the relevant institution or authorised examination body.
